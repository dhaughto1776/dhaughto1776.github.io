Led the team through design, build, and flight of a High Altitude Balloon payload. My role as lead ML engineer: develop ARNIE - Attitude Recognition Neural Intelligence Engine. This was an onboard image classification system trained and deployed to a Raspberry Pi via Tensor Flow Lite. I built the data augmentation and image manipulation pipeline used to train it, alongside assisting with the mechanical and electrical design and development of the payload itself (this included magnetometer calibration and Raspberry Pi camera setup).

Build

ARNIE is a custom CNN, built in TensorFlow using keras, and trained on Google Colab GPUs (specifically the T4 GPU). I built ARNIE's architecture (including a custom cosine loss function for angle wrap handling), the training pipeline, the data augmentation work behind it, and the payload's flight software from scratch.

ARNIE was trained on 10 m Sentinel-2 imagery, with realistic conditions for the time of year of the mission. ARNIE's purpose was attitude recognition based on the orientation of high contrast geological features in imagery from the onboard camera. As such, the model was purposefully overfitted to regions in the area of operation (see the map below). The trained model was quantised and converted to TF Lite for microcontroller inference.

Map of the area covering all four potential launch sites for the 2026 TupperSat mission. Lough Neagh and Lough Erne were treated as two primary targets, around which the Monte-Carlo cropping for training data was focused. The shorelines of these lakes provided the necessary distinct feature for training the model to orient itself.

Results

Team SkyNet had a very successful flight. For the total ~2 hour flight, SkyNet was the only team to remain in constant operation and contact with ground stations. ARNIE inference was successful in 243/243 attempts on the compute-constrained microcomputer, and the TupperSat was successfully recovered post-flight.

The only facet that didn't go to plan was ARNIE's attitude recognition. Due to haze and significant swinging and rotational motion of the payload during flight, the images provided to the CNN differed massively to what it was trained on, so it collapsed to guessing the most commonly encountered angles in the training data. It was suspected this would happen during development, but due to time constraints between CDR and FAR, there was little that could be done to correct.

That being said, ground testing validated the model could learn and perform attitude recognition, and flight testing validated that the end-to-end system could work as intended, leaving only this data discrepancy as the gap to be bridged. If I were to do this project again, with a larger budget, I would run data collection flights for more informed data augmentation.

ARNIE's performance following 50 epochs of training. As can be seen, the model was adept at predicting the heading of the payload based on the high contrast geological features visible to it (In this case, Lough Erne in Northern Ireland).

Telemetry data (Altitude, velocity and acceleration) over the course of the ~2 hr flight

Retrospective

Technically, this project taught me the importance of proper data augmentation. While the pipeline I built was extensive, the depth of the augmentation was vastly insufficient for capturing actual flight conditions for making ARNIE work (which lead to it performing poorly at in-flight attitude classification, despite its strong performance in lab testing).

From a team perspective, this project taught me about the importance of skill diversity. My team was diverse and adept at both hardware and software design. Between the body of the TupperSat, the on-board software, ARNIE, and the sensors, without a broad and varying skill set between us, the mission never would've got off the ground.

I learned an immeasurable amount from working with my team members, and developed close friendships with them. I also learned a lot about conflict resolution, coordinating a technical team while working under stressful conditions, mission planning and accountability tracking.

3. Capstone Research — Type Ia Supernova Progenitor Systems

role
Astrophysics research
dataset
ZTF, ATLAS, NIST
duration
Sept–Dec, 2024
supervisor
Dr. Kate Maguire, TCD

Investigated progenitor systems of Type Ia supernovae using Zwicky Transient Facility data, applying Gaussian process regression to light curve analysis alongside spectral analysis. Specifically, I investigated 03FG and 02ES subtypes. Both differ in terms of size, but exhibit a characteristic 'bump' in flux at the start of their lightcurve.

Method

GP Regression was applied to address gaps in SNe lightcurve data, and to assess the presence of the characteristic flux bump in ZTF data. Piscola was applied to ATLAS data to calculate the change in brightness post explosion. Spectral analysis was performed to investigate the pseudo equivalent width of particular carbon features. The trends for both subtypes were compared, to assess the similarity between them. Tool suite included python, george and piscola for lightcurve modelling.

The lightcurve showing the lead up and explosion of ZTF19abkeuto.

The characteristic flux bump of interest can be seen occuring 20 days prior to peak brightness. It is believed that this bump is caused by interaction of the ejected SNe material with an outer disk of ionised carbon.

Findings

Showed a correlation between the supernovae, but data sparsity for both subtypes meant that there was insufficient evidence for the result to be considered significant. Provided weak support for existing and future research.
